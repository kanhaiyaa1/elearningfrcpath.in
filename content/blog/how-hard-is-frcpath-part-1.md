Candidates preparing for the Royal College of Pathologists examination ask one question more than any other: how hard is FRCPath Part 1? The honest answer is that it is demanding, but it is a fair examination that rewards a particular kind of preparation. It is not designed to trick anyone. It tests whether you can apply histopathology knowledge to short clinical scenarios, quickly and accurately, across the whole curriculum.

This post describes the verified format of the histopathology Part 1, what is and is not known about pass rates, the reasons we see candidates lose marks, and a study plan you can adapt. Where a figure is our own estimate or a candidate report instead of an official number, we say so.

---

## What FRCPath Part 1 Histopathology Looks Like

According to the College's histopathology examination page, Part 1 is made up of 125 multiple-choice questions, a mixture of one-best-answer (single best answer) and extended-matching formats, and some questions include images. The paper lasts 3 hours. It tests understanding of histopathology and cytopathology, autopsy practice and the basic sciences that underpin pathology, including molecular biology.

That is about 86 seconds per question, which is the first source of difficulty. You cannot reason slowly through every stem. You need enough recall and pattern recognition to answer common questions in under a minute, so that you bank time for the multi-step ones.

From the Autumn 2026 sitting, the exam is delivered on a computer through the Risr platform in British Council test centres. The College states that the syllabus, blueprint, format, number of questions, standard-setting process and pass standard are unchanged. Only the delivery has changed. The Autumn 2026 international centres included New Delhi, Karachi, Amman, Singapore, Colombo and Yangon. If you have only practised from books, spend some of your preparation on screen, where you can click an image to enlarge it and flag questions to return to later.

Eligibility, as reported in 2024, required a medical degree, at least one year of specialty training and educational supervisor confirmation. Check the current regulations, because eligibility wording can be updated.

## What We Know About Pass Rates

The College publishes examination performance reports after each sitting. According to the College's description, these give summary information about FRCPath performance broken down by specialty, by UK and international centres, and by attempt. The most recent reports listed when we checked covered the Spring 2026 sitting. That is the best source for a real pass rate, and we suggest you read the latest histopathology figures yourself before drawing conclusions.

We do not quote a specific pass rate in this post, because we could not verify one from the reports. Candidate reports on forums and in training groups often suggest that roughly 50 to 60 percent pass at the first attempt. Treat that as anecdotal. It varies by diet, it is not an official statistic, and it may differ for UK trainees and overseas candidates, which is exactly the split the College reports show.

One official rule is useful for planning. RCPath policy allows four attempts at each part of the FRCPath examinations. A candidate who has failed on four occasions cannot re-enter unless Council grants permission. That is a ceiling on attempts, and it also means each failed sitting is both a delay and an extra fee. Our post on [FRCPath exam fees](/blog/frcpath-exam-fees/) lays out what a repeat sitting costs.

## Why Candidates Lose Marks

These are our observations from teaching candidates, not official findings.

### 1. Too little timed question practice

Reading a standard textbook gives you the knowledge. Answering 125 questions in 3 hours is a different skill. Candidates who read thoroughly but practise few timed blocks tend to run short of time in the last third of the paper. We suggest several thousand practice questions across the preparation period. That is our own recommendation, not a College figure, and quality of review matters more than the raw count.

### 2. Neglecting extended-matching questions

Extended-matching questions give one long option list for several stems. Candidates who only drill single-best-answer questions often handle them poorly. Read the stem first, form your own answer, and only then scan the list.

### 3. Weak breadth

Common carcinomas are well drilled. Marks are lost in uncommon tumours, soft tissue and bone, dermatopathology, neuropathology and cytopathology. The blueprint covers the whole curriculum, so a gap in one area costs you directly.

### 4. Out-of-date classification

Questions follow current WHO classification. If you studied from a 2016 text, the shift to the WHO 5th edition and the 2021 CNS classification will cost you. Our guide to [molecular-clinical integration](/blog/molecular-clinical-integration-pathology-neet-ss-frcpath-2026/) explains how classification has moved from histology-first to molecular-first in several organ systems.

### 5. Images treated as an afterthought

Some questions include images. If you rarely look at slides, you will recognise less under time pressure. Build image practice into every week.

## Topics Candidates Usually Find Hardest

This ranking is our faculty impression, not survey data.

| Area | Why it feels hard |
|---|---|
| Soft tissue and bone tumours | Large classification, overlapping morphology, many fusion genes |
| Haematopathology | Entity list, immunophenotype and cytogenetics together |
| Neuropathology | Integrated diagnoses with molecular parameters |
| Gynaecological pathology | Many tumour subtypes, recent endometrial molecular typing |
| Molecular pathology | Mutations, fusions and testing methods tested in clinical context |
| Dermatopathology | Melanocytic spectrum and rare adnexal tumours |
| Cytopathology | Less exposure during routine MD training |

Topics that candidates find easier usually include general pathology principles and the common carcinomas, because they are covered repeatedly in training.

## A Realistic Four-Month Plan

This plan assumes two to three hours a day and a candidate already working in histopathology. Adjust it to your clinical load.

| Month | Focus | Question practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | General pathology and the common systems, with notes on WHO changes | Short topic-wise sets, reviewed the same day |
| 2 | Systemic pathology organ by organ, including uncommon tumours | Topic blocks of increasing length |
| 3 | Haematopathology, neuropathology, cytology and weak areas | Mixed blocks, including extended-matching sets |
| 4 | Timed full-length papers on screen, image review, error log | Full 125-question timed blocks |

In the last month, run at least a few complete timed sets on a computer, then spend more time on the error log than on new material. Our [FRCPath Part 1 and Part 2 strategy guide](/blog/frcpath-exam-strategy-guide-part1-part2/) covers the full pathway, and the [Part 1 histopathology course](https://elearningfrcpath.com/frcpath-part-1-histopathology-course) follows a similar sequence. ## How Does It Compare With NEET-SS?

The two exams test different things. FRCPath Part 1 is a single three-hour paper that blends recall with short clinical reasoning across histopathology and cytopathology. NEET-SS is a national selection exam with its own question number, marking scheme and competition, set out in the NBEMS notice for each year. We have not listed their formats side by side because the details change by year, and you should read each official document. If you plan to attempt both, the [NEET-SS and FRCPath dual plan](/blog/frcpath-autumn-2026-application-neet-ss-dual-plan/) shows how the timelines fit. The sister site NEET SS Pathology also publishes a [comparison with FRCPath](https://neetsspathology.com/vs-frcpath).

## Exam Pearls and a Mnemonic

**Mnemonic: TIME** (what to protect on exam day)

- **T**iming: about 86 seconds per question, so flag and move.
- **I**mages: enlarge and describe before choosing.
- **M**atching questions: decide your answer before reading the option list.
- **E**rror log: review mistakes by cause, not just by topic.

**Pearls**

- Part 1 has 125 questions in 3 hours, so speed on common material matters.
- Delivery moved to computer in British Council centres from Autumn 2026, and content did not change.
- The College publishes performance reports by specialty, centre type and attempt.
- Four attempts per part is the standard limit before Council permission is required.

## Practice MCQs

**Q1.** A core biopsy of a breast carcinoma shows ER-positive, PR-negative disease, and HER2 immunohistochemistry scores 2+. What is the next step in HER2 assessment according to ASCO/CAP guidance?

- A) Report HER2 as positive
- B) Report HER2 as negative
- C) Perform reflex in situ hybridisation on the same specimen
- D) Repeat immunohistochemistry on a metastatic site only

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: C — Perform reflex in situ hybridisation on the same specimen**

An IHC score of 2+ is equivocal. ASCO/CAP guidance requires reflex testing by in situ hybridisation, or repeat testing on another specimen, to resolve HER2 status. Scores of 3+ are positive and 0 or 1+ are negative. Option D is not the guideline step.

</details>

**Q2.** A lymph node biopsy shows a diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with rearrangements of both MYC and BCL2 on FISH. Under the WHO 2022 haematolymphoid classification, which is the correct diagnosis?

- A) Burkitt lymphoma
- B) Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, NOS
- C) High-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements
- D) Primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: C — High-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements**

The WHO 5th edition uses this designation for large B-cell lymphomas with both MYC and BCL2 rearrangements, a category formerly called double-hit lymphoma. It is not DLBCL NOS (B), and Burkitt lymphoma (A) lacks BCL2 rearrangement. Primary mediastinal lymphoma (D) is defined by site and phenotype.

</details>

**Q3.** A lung adenocarcinoma in a 45-year-old never-smoker shows an ALK rearrangement on immunohistochemistry confirmed by FISH. Which therapy class is most appropriate?

- A) EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor
- B) ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor
- C) BRAF inhibitor
- D) PARP inhibitor

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: B — ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor**

ALK-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma is treated with ALK inhibitors such as alectinib. EGFR inhibitors (A) apply to EGFR-mutant tumours, BRAF inhibitors (C) to BRAF V600E tumours, and PARP inhibitors (D) to BRCA-related disease.

</details>

## FAQs

**How hard is FRCPath Part 1 for an Indian MD Pathology graduate?**

It is demanding but achievable. The exam follows the UK histopathology curriculum, so expect some content that is emphasised differently from Indian training, such as UK reporting practice and autopsy-related basics. Indian MD graduates usually have strong morphology. The common gaps are timed question practice, cytopathology and the most recent classification updates. A structured four-month plan with regular timed blocks closes most of those gaps.

**What is the pass rate for FRCPath Part 1?**

The College publishes examination performance reports broken down by specialty, UK and international centres, and attempt, and the latest listed when we checked was Spring 2026. We did not verify a specific histopathology figure. Candidate reports suggest roughly 50 to 60 percent pass at the first attempt, but that is anecdotal. Read the official report for the current sitting.

**How many questions are in FRCPath Part 1 histopathology?**

The College describes Part 1 as 125 multiple-choice questions, mixing one-best-answer and extended-matching formats, to be completed in 3 hours. Some questions include images. From Autumn 2026 the paper is taken on a computer in a British Council test centre, with unchanged content and pass standard.

**How many attempts do I get at Part 1?**

RCPath policy allows four attempts at each part of the FRCPath examinations. A candidate who has failed on four occasions is not permitted to re-enter unless Council grants permission. Because each sitting carries its own fee, repeat attempts also add cost, so plan to be ready at the first or second sitting.

**How long should I prepare for FRCPath Part 1?**

For a candidate already working in histopathology, three to four months of structured study at two to three hours a day is a reasonable plan, with the last month given to timed full-length papers. Candidates with a heavy clinical load or less exposure to cytopathology and uncommon tumours may need longer. Start earlier than you think you need, because the application window closes months before the sitting.

## Key Takeaways

- FRCPath Part 1 histopathology has 125 one-best-answer and extended-matching questions in 3 hours, around 86 seconds a question.
- The exam moved to British Council test centres from Autumn 2026, with content, standard setting and pass standard unchanged.
- The College publishes performance reports by specialty, centre type and attempt. Use them instead of guesses for pass rates.
- Failure usually traces to limited timed practice, weak breadth, outdated classification and neglected images.
- Four attempts per part is the limit before Council permission is needed.
- A four-month plan with a timed on-screen final month suits most working candidates.
