This NEET SS Pathology study guide is built around one observation from our teaching: most candidates do not fail for lack of effort. They fail because they study in the wrong order and spend too little time on the topics that the exam now rewards, namely molecular pathology, immunohistochemistry and the current WHO classifications. What follows is a six-month sequence you can adapt to your own start date.

Read the official NBEMS information bulletin for the exact question number, duration and marking scheme of your year, because these can change and we do not quote them here. For a shorter plan and the syllabus map, the sister site has a [NEET SS pathology study plan](https://neetsspathology.com/neet-ss-pathology-study-plan-2026) and a [syllabus page](https://neetsspathology.com/neet-ss-pathology-syllabus-2026). This guide covers the content sequence, the revision tables and the mistakes to avoid.

---

## What the Exam Tests

NEET-SS sits above the postgraduate entrance level. A question is less likely to ask you to name a cell and more likely to ask which marker, translocation or classification decision follows from a described case. That shapes how you should study.

- **Application, not recall.** You need the reason behind a fact, such as why an IHC panel separates two lymphomas.
- **Molecular and IHC content.** Many questions pair a morphology with a mutation, fusion or marker.
- **Current classification.** Questions follow the WHO 5th edition Blue Books and the 2021 CNS classification, so older editions can mislead you.

Our post on [molecular-clinical integration](/blog/molecular-clinical-integration-pathology-neet-ss-frcpath-2026/) explains why this shift matters across NEET-SS, FRCPath and INI-CET.

## The Six-Month Plan at a Glance

| Months | Focus | Output you should have |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | General pathology and basic molecular concepts | Clean notes on neoplasia and haemodynamics |
| 2 | Molecular pathology and IHC tables | Memorised mutation and marker lists |
| 3 | Systemic oncopathology, part 1 | Lymphoma, lung, GI and breast revised |
| 4 | Systemic oncopathology, part 2 | Gynae, CNS, skin, head and neck, bone and soft tissue, thyroid |
| 5 | Haematopathology and coagulation depth | Leukaemia, lymphoma entities and coagulation tables |
| 6 | Question practice and weak areas | Timed blocks, previous papers, error log |

If you have less than six months, compress months 1 to 2 and protect month 6, because timed practice is the one phase that cannot be skipped.

## Months 1 and 2: Foundation, Molecular Pathology and IHC

### General pathology, in application form

Revise cell injury and death, inflammation, neoplasia and haemodynamics from a standard postgraduate text. Do not skim. Mark tables and ask for each topic what a question stem might describe. Pigments, amyloid, granulomas and the molecular basis of cancer are common starting points.

### Molecular pathology list

Learn mutations and fusions as a lookup list by tumour type.

| Alteration | Typical associations |
|---|---|
| BRAF V600E | Papillary thyroid carcinoma, melanoma, hairy cell leukaemia, Langerhans cell histiocytosis |
| IDH1 and IDH2 | Diffuse glioma, AML, cholangiocarcinoma |
| KRAS | Pancreatic, colorectal and lung adenocarcinomas |
| ALK rearrangement | Lung adenocarcinoma, ALK-positive ALCL, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour |
| KIT | GIST, mastocytosis, core-binding-factor AML, seminoma |
| EGFR exon 19 deletion, exon 21 L858R | Lung adenocarcinoma |
| BCR::ABL1 | CML, Philadelphia-positive ALL |
| MYC rearrangement | Burkitt lymphoma, high-grade B-cell lymphoma |
| SS18::SSX | Synovial sarcoma |
| EWSR1::FLI1 | Ewing sarcoma |

WHO now writes fusion genes with a double colon. Expect to see both the older hyphen and the newer notation.

### IHC lookup tables

| Question | Typical positive markers | Typical negative markers |
|---|---|---|
| Lung adenocarcinoma | TTF-1, napsin A, CK7 | p40 |
| Lung squamous carcinoma | p40, p63, CK5/6 | TTF-1 |
| Mesothelioma | Calretinin, WT1, D2-40, CK5/6 | MOC31, Ber-EP4 |
| Metastatic adenocarcinoma in effusion | MOC31, Ber-EP4 | Calretinin, WT1 |
| Hepatocellular carcinoma | HepPar-1, arginase-1, glypican-3 | CK7 usually negative |
| Cholangiocarcinoma | CK7, CK19 | HepPar-1 |
| Clear cell renal carcinoma | CAIX, PAX8, CD10 | CK7 usually negative |
| Papillary renal carcinoma | AMACR, CK7, PAX8 | CAIX usually negative |
| Urothelial carcinoma | GATA3, p63, CK7, CK20 | Not applicable |

Revise these tables every week. They appear inside clinical vignettes and are rarely asked as a direct recall question.

## Months 3 and 4: Systemic Oncopathology

Go organ by organ. For each, follow the same chain: WHO classification, diagnostic criteria, IHC, molecular features and staging or grading. The order below reflects exam frequency in our experience, not an official weightage.

1. **Lymphomas.** Learn the entity list with immunophenotype and cytogenetics.
2. **Lung.** IASLC adenocarcinoma patterns, and EGFR, ALK, ROS1 and PD-L1 testing.
3. **Gastrointestinal.** Colorectal mismatch repair and MSI, gastric HER2, GIST with KIT and PDGFRA.
4. **Breast.** Nottingham grade, ER, PR, HER2 including HER2-low, and intrinsic subtypes. See our post on [HER2-low and ultralow breast cancer](/blog/her2-low-ultralow-breast-cancer-pathology-frcpath-neet-ss-2026/).
5. **Gynaecological.** Cervical HPV-associated and independent tumours, endometrial molecular classes including POLE, and ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma with BRCA.
6. **CNS.** The 2021 classification with IDH, 1p/19q, CDKN2A/B, TERT and related parameters.
7. **Skin.** Melanoma staging and Breslow thickness, basal cell versus squamous cell carcinoma.
8. **Head and neck.** Salivary gland tumours and nasopharyngeal carcinoma with EBV.
9. **Bone and soft tissue.** Fusion-driven sarcomas as a table.
10. **Thyroid.** Papillary, follicular and anaplastic carcinoma, with the molecular drivers listed above.

Use the WHO Blue Books for the organ systems you find hardest. Our posts on [molecular markers in oncopathology](/blog/molecular-markers-oncopath-neet-ss-frcpath-2026/) and [mismatch repair deficiency](/blog/mismatch-repair-deficiency-pathology-frcpath-neet-ss/) cover two of the most tested clusters.

## Month 5: Haematopathology and Coagulation

**Leukaemia.** For AML, know the blast threshold ideas, the genetic abnormalities that define entities and the cytogenetic risk groups. t(8;21), inv(16) and t(15;17) are the classic favourable abnormalities. For MDS, know SF3B1-associated disease. For CML, know BCR::ABL1 and the phases. For ALL, separate B-ALL from T-ALL and know Philadelphia-positive disease.

**Lymphoma.** Group entities as aggressive B-cell (DLBCL with cell-of-origin by the Hans algorithm, Burkitt, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements), indolent B-cell (follicular, marginal zone, CLL/SLL, hairy cell), T-cell (PTCL NOS, AITL with follicular helper markers, ALCL ALK-positive and negative) and Hodgkin (nodular lymphocyte predominant versus classic).

**Coagulation.** Revise PT and aPTT and the pathways they test, von Willebrand disease types (type 2B has increased low-dose ristocetin response), heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with anti-PF4 antibodies and non-heparin anticoagulants, and a comparison table for DIC, TTP and HUS.

## Month 6: Timed Practice and Weak-Area Repair

Spend the first half of the month on topic-wise blocks to find weak chapters, and the second half on timed full papers. Review each wrong answer by cause: knowledge gap, misread stem or time pressure. Keep an error log and revisit it every few days. Our posts on [PYQ-driven prep](/blog/pyq-driven-pathology-prep-neet-ss-2026/), [section-wise time management](/blog/neet-ss-2026-section-wise-time-management-strategy/) and the [30-day last-month plan](/blog/last-month-section-wise-30-day-neet-ss-frcpath-pathology-plan/) give detail for this phase.

We suggest a few thousand practice questions across the whole six months. That is our recommendation, not an official figure. A topic-wise bank such as the [NEET-SS pathology course](https://elearningfrcpath.com/neet-ss-pathology) and the [pathology MCQ bank](https://elearningfrcpath.com/pathology-mcq-2026) can serve as the question source.

## Mistakes That Cost Ranks

1. **Using older WHO editions.** Entity names and criteria changed in haematolymphoid, CNS and several other systems.
2. **Leaving molecular pathology late.** It is the single largest source of lost marks in our experience.
3. **Reading without questions.** Reading alone does not train application.
4. **Skipping cytopathology.** It is easy to ignore and easy to score on.
5. **Revising IHC once.** Tables need weekly repetition.

---

## Exam Pearls and a Mnemonic

**Mnemonic: MIST** (the four tables to revise weekly)

- **M**utations by tumour
- **I**HC panels
- **S**taging and grading rules
- **T**ranslocations and fusions

**Pearls**

- Link every morphology to its marker, its molecular driver and its management consequence.
- Use the WHO 5th edition and the 2021 CNS classification, not older texts.
- Keep an error log from month 3 onward.

## Practice MCQs

**Q1.** A lung biopsy shows an adenocarcinoma. Which immunohistochemistry result best supports a primary lung origin?

- A) TTF-1 and napsin A positive
- B) p40 and CK5/6 positive
- C) Calretinin and WT1 positive
- D) HepPar-1 positive

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: A — TTF-1 and napsin A positive**

TTF-1 and napsin A support primary lung adenocarcinoma. p40 and CK5/6 (B) favour squamous differentiation. Calretinin and WT1 (C) favour mesothelioma. HepPar-1 (D) favours hepatocellular lineage.

</details>

**Q2.** A lymph node shows a nodular follicular pattern. The cells are CD20 positive, CD10 positive and BCL6 positive, and BCL2 is strongly positive. Which translocation is expected?

- A) t(8;14)
- B) t(14;18)
- C) t(11;14)
- D) t(15;17)

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: B — t(14;18)**

Follicular lymphoma typically carries t(14;18) with IGH::BCL2 rearrangement, causing BCL2 overexpression. t(8;14) is Burkitt lymphoma, t(11;14) is mantle cell lymphoma and t(15;17) is acute promyelocytic leukaemia.

</details>

**Q3.** A Hodgkin lymphoma biopsy shows large neoplastic cells that are CD20 positive, CD45 positive, OCT2 positive and negative for CD15 and CD30. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Classic Hodgkin lymphoma, nodular sclerosis
- B) Nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma
- C) Anaplastic large cell lymphoma
- D) T-cell/histiocyte-rich large B-cell lymphoma

<details>
<summary>View Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: B — Nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma**

LP cells express B-cell markers such as CD20, CD45 and OCT2 and are typically negative for CD15 and CD30, unlike classic Hodgkin lymphoma. Anaplastic large cell lymphoma is CD30 positive. T-cell/histiocyte-rich large B-cell lymphoma is a close differential and is separated by architecture and clinical context.

</details>

## FAQs

**How should I start studying for NEET SS Pathology?**

Begin with general pathology and basic molecular concepts, then build a molecular and IHC lookup list before you move to organ systems. This order gives you the vocabulary that systemic oncopathology depends on. Keep notes short and tabular, and attach a small set of practice questions to each topic as you finish it.

**How many months do I need for NEET SS Pathology?**

Six months is a comfortable plan for most MD graduates, with the last month reserved for timed practice. Candidates with less time can compress the foundation months but should not skip timed papers. Check the NBEMS bulletin for exam dates, then work backward to set your own milestones.

**Which books should I use for NEET SS Pathology?**

Use a standard postgraduate pathology text for foundation, the WHO 5th edition volumes for classification in the organ systems you find hardest, a surgical pathology reference for oncopathology depth, and a haematology text for leukaemia, lymphoma and coagulation. Previous-year papers and a structured question bank complete the set.

**How important is molecular pathology for NEET SS?**

It is among the most important areas, because many questions pair a morphology with a mutation, fusion or marker and ask for the consequence. We do not give a percentage because no official weightage is published. Treat mutations, fusions and IHC panels as lookup lists and revise them every week.

**Can I prepare for NEET SS and FRCPath together?**

Many candidates do, because the core systemic pathology, WHO classification and molecular content overlap substantially. The question formats differ, so keep separate timed practice for each. Our posts on the [NEET-SS and FRCPath dual plan](/blog/frcpath-autumn-2026-application-neet-ss-dual-plan/) and [NEET-SS versus INI-SS](/blog/neet-ss-vs-neet-ini-ss-2026-pathology-entrance-comparison/) set out timelines.

## Key Takeaways

- Study in this order: general pathology, molecular and IHC tables, systemic oncopathology, haematopathology, then timed practice.
- Revise mutation, IHC and fusion tables weekly, because they appear inside clinical vignettes.
- Use the WHO 5th edition and the 2021 CNS classification.
- Keep an error log and review mistakes by cause.
- Confirm the question number, duration and marking scheme in the NBEMS bulletin for your year.
